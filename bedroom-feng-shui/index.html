Bed placement: which wall, and why

Last updated 10 October 2026

The head of the bed goes against a solid wall, where you can see the bedroom door without lying in line with it. Which wall that should be depends on two things: the flying stars in that part of the floor plan, and the personal directions of whoever sleeps there.

What makes a good wall for the head of the bed?

Five things, and they are ordinary enough to check in a minute:

  • A solid wall behind the head, not a window.
  • The door visible from the bed, without the bed lying straight in line with it.
  • No bathroom or toilet on the other side of that wall.
  • No mirror on the wall the bed faces.
  • Stars in that sector that are not among the unsettled pairings.

Where those pull against each other, the stars decide first and the personal directions second. That order matters, and the next section explains why.

Why does the bed point away from the door?

A bed whose feet point straight at the door is the one placement traditional practice is most consistent about avoiding. Flying Stars treats it as ruling the wall out rather than merely counting against it.

A door on the same wall as the head is weaker but not ruled out: the trouble there is that nobody in bed can see who comes in. The wall that scores best is the one where the door is in view but not aligned with the bed.

Can the bed go under a window?

It is weighed against the wall rather than ruling it out. A solid headboard and closed curtains answer most of what the objection is about, and ruling out every window wall would leave a great many ordinary bedrooms with no wall anyone could recommend.

A bathroom or toilet on the other side of the wall behind the head is treated more strictly, and rules that wall out.

What about a mirror facing the bed?

Traditional practice widely avoids a mirror that reflects the bed, and many practitioners treat it as one of the first things to move in a bedroom. Flying Stars weighs it.

Mark which walls carry a mirror and the reading counts it against any wall that would put the bed facing one, with the line “a mirror on the facing wall would reflect the bed”. It is scored against the wall the bed faces, not the wall the mirror hangs on: a mirror behind the headboard reflects nothing of the bed, and changes nothing.

Like the window, it counts against a wall rather than ruling it out. A mirror can be moved, rehung or faced away, which a window cannot, so it should never be the reason a room is left with no wall worth recommending.

Does my kua number decide which way the bed faces?

It contributes, at half the weight of the stars. Your four best directions come from your kua number, and for sleeping the health direction leads, with success, love and personal growth close behind.

The reason personal directions come second is practical. An early version of this scoring weighed a single star instead of the pair, and put a child’s bed in a sector carrying one of the worst combinations simply because it happened to be her success direction. A difficult pairing now loses to any other wall on the stars alone, even when the other wall is in the sleeper’s weakest direction.

What if two people share the bed and suit different walls?

The wall is judged partly on the person it suits least, not only on the average of the two. A wall that is ideal for one partner and poor for the other does not get passed off as a fair compromise, and the app says which direction belongs to which person so the trade-off is visible rather than buried in a score.

Does the room's own chart matter more than the bedroom rules?

They are read together. The sector of the floor plan the bedroom sits in carries its mountain, water and period stars, worked out from the building’s facing direction and the period it was built in. Those are what make the same bedroom layout right in one home and wrong in another.

This is why generic bedroom advice can only go so far. “Head to the east” is not a rule that holds across buildings; which wall is good depends on numbers that are particular to your home.

What should I do if no wall looks good?

It happens, and it is worth knowing rather than forcing. Small rooms, a door and a window on opposite walls, or an en-suite behind the one long wall can leave every option carrying something. The app ranks the walls rather than presenting one answer: it gives the reasons for the wall it recommends, names any wall it has ruled out and why, and offers up to two others worth using.

When every wall has a problem it says so, rather than dressing the least bad one up as a good choice. That is the sentence worth waiting for, and it is the one most generic bedroom advice never gets to.

A floor plan also cannot show everything: the land, the surroundings, the people. For important decisions, an experienced practitioner’s eye is worth having.
